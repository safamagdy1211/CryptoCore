WHAT IS DES?

DES (Data Encryption Standard, 1977) is a symmetric block cipher adopted by NIST. It encrypts 64-bit blocks using a 56-bit key through 16 identical Feistel rounds. It was the global encryption standard until AES replaced it in 2001.

KEY SCHEDULE

The 64-bit key has 8 parity bits removed by PC-1 permutation, leaving 56 bits split into two 28-bit halves C and D. Each round, both halves are left-shifted by 1 or 2 bits, then PC-2 selects 48 bits to form the round subkey Ki.

FEISTEL STRUCTURE

The 64-bit plaintext block undergoes an Initial Permutation (IP), then splits into 32-bit halves L₀ and R₀. Each of 16 rounds computes:

Lᵢ = Rᵢ₋₁
Rᵢ = Lᵢ₋₁ ⊕ F(Rᵢ₋₁, Kᵢ)

THE F-FUNCTION

F takes 32-bit R and 48-bit subkey K. It expands R to 48 bits (E), XORs with K, passes through 8 S-boxes (each 6→4 bits, providing non-linearity), then applies a final P-permutation.

F(R,K) = P(S(E(R) ⊕ K))

S-BOXES (HEART OF DES)

The 8 S-boxes provide the only non-linear step. Each S-box takes 6 bits: outer bits select the row (0-3), inner 4 bits select the column (0-15). The table lookup outputs 4 bits. This substitution makes DES resistant to linear analysis.

WHY DES IS WEAK TODAY

The 56-bit key space (2⁵⁶ ≈ 72 quadrillion keys) was broken by brute-force in 1998 in under 23 hours by the EFF DES Cracker. Modern systems use 3DES (triple-DES) or AES-128/256. DES remains essential for understanding modern cipher design.
